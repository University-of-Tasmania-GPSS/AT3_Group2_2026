# Discussion: what explains the contrast, and what remains unresolved?

**Changing glacier geometry and terminus setting provide the clearest interpretation of the contrast between Brown and Stephenson.** Stephenson lost much of its extensive lower tongue, while Brown's surveyed lagoon records retreat through a water-filled part of its former footprint. These observations support an interpretation in which the landscape and changing glacier shape influence how retreat progresses. The contribution of buried geology, individual bed highs and glacier–water processes remains unresolved, and climate forcing has not been separated from those influences.

The strongest agreement is between the outline history, the loss of Stephenson's low-elevation footprint and its changing width. Together they show that recent retreat occurs through a different glacier shape from the earlier broad tongue. Other comparisons qualify the explanation. Faster retained flow at Stephenson adds contemporary context, but the unequal coverage and observation dates limit its historical interpretation. ## Changing geometry helps reconcile different retreat measures

### Area loss, front movement and the disappearing lower tongue

Stephenson's recent front-retreat rate increased from **61.31 to 103.48 m/year**, while proportional area loss decreased from **1.84% to 0.40%/year**. Brown shows the opposite combination: front retreat decreased from **19.09 to 11.90 m/year**, while proportional area loss increased from **0.42% to 0.61%/year**. These compare 2014–2019 with 2019–2026.

A front moving through a narrower tongue can retreat farther while removing less area. Stephenson's width profiles support this geometric contribution to the apparently conflicting trends. Brown's glacier-wide area change can also include lateral contraction that a single front position does not capture. Route and front shape affect terminus measurements, as assessed by Lea et al. (2014). The geometry reconciles the measures, while the causes of ice loss still require explanation.

The former low-elevation footprint also matters. Stephenson began with **16.01 km² below 300 m**, compared with **1.70 km² at Brown**, using the common DEM. Glacier area distributed across elevations can affect sensitivity to climate forcing (McGrath et al., 2017), but the thresholds here describe footprint positions. They do not reconstruct historical surface heights or distinguish accumulation from ablation. A declining recent area-loss rate cannot establish slower thinning, lower mass loss or general stabilisation.

### Flow as contemporary context

Stephenson's retained lower-band median speed, **266.52 m/year**, is substantially higher than Brown's **39.57 m/year**. However, the respective coverage is **85.41% and 38.46%**. Brown's median represents a restricted subset, and excluded or missing cells cannot be assigned zero motion.

Ice speed describes transport through a glacier. Front position also depends on losses at its margin, so faster flow alone does not establish faster retreat (Truffer and Motyka, 2016). The 2016–2022 observations provide a flow context for the remaining glaciers, without measuring motion throughout the historical intervals or directly at the 2026 fronts. Velocity also contributes to the thickness estimation (Millan et al., 2022), limiting the independence of relationships between flow and the modelled bed.

(discussion-terminus)=
## Terminus setting and Brown's lagoon basin

### What the observed basin establishes

Brown's soundings establish a deep water-filled depression within its former ice footprint. The supported **0.3301 km²** reconstruction includes substantial areas deeper than 30 m and a deepest observed sounding of 56 m (Allison and Thost, 2010). Its complete overlap with the 1947 outline and absence from later outlines place withdrawal from this surveyed area between 1947 and 1988.

Tielidze et al. (2025) describe an early lake-associated retreat phase at Brown followed by land termination, and later, extensive lagoon development at Stephenson. The Brown overlap provides local spatial support for that history. It does not date the formation of the entire lagoon or establish when every part of the glacier lost water contact.

Water contact permits calving and melting below the water surface, whose importance depends on water conditions and glacier geometry (Truffer and Motyka, 2016). A changing terminus setting could therefore alter the processes acting at the front. This remains a conditional explanation: the present evidence does not measure historical calving, water temperature, salinity or subaqueous melt rates. Stephenson's much smaller sounding record also prevents an equivalent comparison of basin geometry.

### A basin, an overdeepening and a possible pinning point

An **overdeepening** is a glacier-excavated depression whose bed rises towards its downstream outlet (Cook and Swift, 2012). Its deepest part and its outlet are distinct features. A sill, ridge or constriction may provide a location that restricts retreat, but demonstrating such a control requires its geometry and the glacier's relationship to it. Bed and fjord geometry influence retreat in other settings, including Greenland tidewater glaciers (Catania et al., 2018); those findings identify mechanisms to investigate here, with different water and glacier conditions.

Brown's depth reconstruction does not resolve the complete outlet or a continuous connection to the inland bed. The 0, 2 and 5 m water-level scenarios preserve the deep central depression but do not bound datum uncertainty. The approximate correction of shoreline GPS heights also does not supply an independently measured 2004 lagoon water level. The basin is established; its complete overdeepened geometry, outlet control and hydraulic connection remain open questions.

(discussion-geology)=
## Geological influence: deposits, substrate and possible bed controls

### Moraine is evidence of deposition

The mapped forelands contain different proportions of moraine and other glacial sediments, but the categories leave material properties unresolved. A moraine records glacial debris deposition. It does not specify sediment strength, thickness, consolidation or the material beneath neighbouring ice. Subglacial sediment behaviour depends on stress, water pressure and material properties (Boulton and Hindmarsh, 1987), which were not measured in this project.

Mapped deposits can also be consequences of former glacier activity. Their association with retreat zones therefore cannot independently establish a geological cause of retreat. This is particularly consequential where Snow/Ice covers almost all recent retreat zones in the source map and unmapped areas occupy large parts of the older zones.

There is a relevant local alternative to simple basin excavation. Kiernan and McConnell (2002) reported that melting of older **ice-cored moraines** contributed to melt-lake expansion at Stephenson. Lake enlargement can thus include change within the surrounding moraine landscape as well as movement of the glacier front. Our geological categories do not identify buried ice, and the available outlines and bathymetry cannot separate those contributions.

### Subglacial terrain remains partly modelled and partly unknown

The screened bed highs narrow the locations worth investigating, particularly Brown's B2. Their sensitivity to route offsets and smoothing, and incomplete lateral support, prevent confirmation of continuous barriers. Persistence counts measure repeatability under selected tests; they are not probabilities that pinning points exist.

The bed estimate combines non-contemporaneous surface and thickness inputs and ends at the valid 2019 ice mask. It therefore omits much of the former coastal tongue where earlier retreat occurred. Archived radar comparisons are local and show uneven model agreement. These checks improve the interpretation of the model, without validating complete valley geometry. The [terrain appendix](appendix.md#appendix-terrain) retains the uncertainty, screening and cross-section evidence.

The geological question consequently remains open: the analysis describes exposed deposits and potential terrain controls, but cannot establish contrasting substrate erodibility beneath Brown and Stephenson.

(discussion-climate)=
## Surface exposure does not isolate climate or melt

Brown's median summer orientation index is slightly higher than Stephenson's (**1.028 versus 0.983**), despite Stephenson's greater historical contraction. Surface orientation alone therefore offers little support for the observed ranking. Clouds, albedo, snow conditions and shadows cast by surrounding terrain remain outside the calculation; such shading can materially alter direct radiation on glacier surfaces (Olson and Rupper, 2019).

A broadly shared regional climate also does not establish identical local forcing. Tielidze et al. (2025) discuss orographic influences on Heard Island's retreat asymmetry. Accumulation, wind redistribution and local energy balance could differ even between these two eastern glaciers. We have not estimated their contributions or removed a climate signal to obtain a geological residual. Geometry and terminus setting are investigated within that climatic context.

### Limitations, responses and consequences

Three limitations most affect the answer. **Different dates and footprints** prevent the layers from representing one simultaneous glacier state. **Unequal support** limits whole-glacier and between-glacier comparisons, especially Brown's lower-band velocity and Stephenson's bathymetry. **Imposed mapping rules and unpropagated boundary uncertainty** limit claims about recent rate changes and upper-glacier stability.

Reporting masks and coverage, preserving gaps, using supported interpolation and testing candidate highs make these restrictions visible. They improve defensibility without recovering missing observations. The two-glacier scope also limits generalisation to the rest of Heard Island. The complete [evidence audit](appendix.md#appendix-evidence) records alternatives and consequences.

### Comparison with other approaches

Tielidze et al. (2025) combined manually mapped 1947, 1988 and 2019 inventories with optical imagery and elevation models. We adapt the spatial comparison to the supplied historical inventories, a 2014 baseline and recent Sentinel-1 mapping. This extends the working record to 2026, while its different boundaries and constraints require comparison at the level of broad patterns.

The field work of Thost and Truffer (2008) included repeated surface observations and radar, supporting estimates of lowering and volume change. Our archive reconstruction supplies local basin and bed evidence, but does not reproduce that repeated field coverage. The main contribution here is the reproducible integration of mapped change, geological preparation, terrain, flow and archived observations, with the limits of each retained through synthesis and the interactive explorer.

(discussion-future)=
## The next measurements that would change the answer

| Open question | Needed measurement | What it would resolve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do basin outlets or bed highs restrict retreat? | Connected bathymetry, outlet surveying and cross-glacier radar tied to one vertical reference | Continuity, sill elevations and the extent of candidate barriers |
| What lies beneath the ice and moraine landscape? | Targeted geological sampling and geophysical observations, including searches for buried ice | Sediment versus bedrock properties and ice-core melting as an alternative process |
| How are geometry and ice transport changing together? | Repeat outlines, surface elevations and velocity over overlapping dates and footprints | Thinning, changing width, ice transport and their relation to front movement |
| How important are climate and water-contact processes? | Local meteorology and accumulation observations, repeat front monitoring, and lagoon water level, temperature and salinity | Conditions and rates needed to test climatic forcing, calving and subaqueous melt |

Recent surveying provides a practical starting point. Williams and Fedrigo (2026) report GNSS control work, radar corner reflectors and drone lidar and bathymetric lidar at Atlas Cove during the 2025–2026 campaigns. Palmer and Dyer (2026) also catalogue public sea-level observations at Atlas Cove and Magnet Point. These records offer material for a future assessment of spatial and vertical control; they were not incorporated here. Their applicability to the eastern glaciers must be checked, and they do not determine Brown Lagoon's 2004 water level.

The interpretation to test is that inherited terrain, changing glacier shape and evolving water contact influence the progression of retreat. The present comparison establishes contrasting histories and identifies where observations could distinguish those mechanisms.
