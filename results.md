# Results: contrasting paths of retreat

**Stephenson lost substantially more of its mapped area than Brown between 1947 and 2026.** The recent intervals show a more complicated contrast: Stephenson's front retreat accelerated while its proportional area loss slowed; Brown's front retreat slowed while its proportional area loss increased. The following comparisons establish that pattern and describe the geometry, mapped materials, flow and field evidence available to interpret it.

## Area loss and front retreat tell different parts of the story

Mapped area change, 1947–2026. **A:** percentage of each glacier's 1947 area remaining. **B:** interval-average area loss in km²/year. **C:** interval-average percentage loss relative to the area at the start of each interval. ### The overall contrast and the recent reversal

Brown lost **40.44%** of its mapped 1947 area by 2026, compared with **68.49%** at Stephenson. Both lost approximately 20% of their original area during 1947–1988; their histories diverged more strongly after 1988. Both had their highest interval-average absolute area-loss rate during **1988–2014**: approximately **0.034 km²/year at Brown** and **0.434 km²/year at Stephenson**.

| Measure | Brown | Stephenson |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cumulative mapped area loss, 1947–2026 | 40.44% | 68.49% |
| Annual proportional area loss, 2014–2019 | 0.42%/year | 1.84%/year |
| Annual proportional area loss, 2019–2026 | 0.61%/year | 0.40%/year |
| Front retreat along the selected route, 2014–2019 | approximately 18–21 m/year | 61.31 m/year |
| Front retreat along the selected route, 2019–2026 | 11.90 m/year | 103.48 m/year |

Proportional area-loss rates use each interval's starting area. Brown had the higher recent proportional rate, but Stephenson still lost more area in absolute terms during 2019–2026: approximately **0.033 km²/year**, compared with **0.020 km²/year** at Brown. Brown also has the higher recent proportional rate when both intervals use the fixed 1947 baseline, so the crossover is not solely an effect of changing denominators.

### Area loss is distinct from front movement

Brown's front retreat stayed around **18–21 m/year from 1947 to 2019**, before slowing in the latest interval. Stephenson's rates were **33.12 m/year during 1947–1988**, **158.07 m/year during 1988–2014**, and then the two recent values shown above. These measure displacement along a fixed route, rather than movement averaged across the entire front.

The greater cumulative contraction at Stephenson agrees with the broad historical contrast reported by Tielidze et al. (2025). Different inventory dates and mapping methods prevent exact numerical equivalence. Neither area loss nor front displacement measures thinning, volume loss or the speed at which ice flows.

Recent outlines exclude advance by construction and inherit the 2014 extent strictly above 1,000 m. Historical dates are inventory labels, while each rate averages change across its full interval. No independent outline-accuracy estimate or propagated uncertainty establishes the statistical significance of the recent rate changes. Supporting measurements and mapping constraints are collected in the [inventory appendix](appendix.md#appendix-inventory).

## How much glacier footprint remained below each elevation?

:::{figure} Figures/09_Synthesis/03_low_elevation_area.png
:label: fig-results-low-elevation
:alt: Three panels showing mapped glacier footprint area below 300, 600 and 900 metres using the same DEM for every inventory date.
:width: 100%
:align: center

Mapped footprint area below **A: 300 m**, **B: 600 m** and **C: 900 m**, using the same 2002 DEM for all outlines. Only cells with valid elevation are counted. Thresholds are nested: the area below 600 m includes the area below 300 m. Panels share an area scale. Changes describe the positions of mapped footprints over that common terrain dataset, rather than reconstructed surface heights at each date. Outlines: Donoghue and Harris (2021) and this project; DEM: Brolsma and Smith (2008).
:::

Stephenson's mapped footprint below 300 m decreased from **16.01 km² in 1947** to **11.04 km² in 1988** and **0.54 km² in 2014**. No valid DEM cells below 300 m were counted within its 2026 outline. Brown's corresponding areas were **1.70, 0.72, 0.22 and 0.11 km²**. Both lost most of their former lowest-elevation footprint, but Stephenson's was initially much larger.

By 2026, Brown retained **1.05 km² below 600 m** and **2.64 km² below 900 m**; Stephenson retained **1.73 km²** and **4.00 km²**, respectively. Below-600 m area represented **33.0% of Brown's footprint with valid elevation**, compared with **21.4% at Stephenson**. Stephenson therefore retained more low-elevation area in absolute terms, but a smaller proportion of its remaining footprint.

These results document the removal of Stephenson's extensive lower tongue, consistent with the historical geometry described by Tielidze et al. (2025). The width profiles below show the accompanying change in glacier shape. Valid DEM coverage is approximately 99–100%, but the use of one older DEM means this calculation does not measure surface lowering or present-day ice elevation. The thresholds also do not identify accumulation or ablation boundaries.

## What the exposed geological map can tell us

:::{figure} Figures/09_Synthesis/02_geology_by_retreat_zone.png
:label: fig-results-geology
:alt: Geological source-map categories beneath selected glacier outlines, with bars showing their proportions in retreat zones and the remaining 2026 footprints.
:width: 100%
:align: center

Mapped surface categories within the analysed glacier footprints. **A–B:** source-map classes and selected glacier outlines. **C–D:** category proportions within successive retreat zones and the 2026 remaining footprint. Percentages use the full zone area, including Snow/Ice and unmapped locations. Areas beside the bars are gross spatial loss for an interval, or remaining area for 2026. Map panels have different scales. Categories describe the geological source map, rather than reconstructed materials at each historical date. Geological source attribution: Fox et al. (2023); outlines: Donoghue and Harris (2021) and this project.
:::

Mapped glacial deposits occur in both forelands, with different proportions and substantial gaps in coverage. In the **1947–1988 retreat zone**, Brown has **42.6% moraine** and **53.9% unmapped area**. Stephenson has **26.3% moraine**, **49.6% unconsolidated glacial sediments** and **21.2% unmapped area**.

For **1988–2014**, moraine occupies **70.8% of Brown's retreat zone**, compared with **22.4% at Stephenson**; Stephenson also has **8.9% unconsolidated glacial sediments** and **59.9% unmapped area**. The zones differ greatly in size: approximately **0.87 km² at Brown** and **11.29 km² at Stephenson**. Consequently, Stephenson has more mapped moraine in absolute area—approximately **2.53 km²**, compared with **0.62 km²** at Brown—despite the smaller percentage.

More than **99.9% of both 2019–2026 retreat zones** is classified as **Snow/Ice** in the geological source map. That classification leaves the newly exposed substrate unresolved. Unmapped areas likewise cannot be assigned to a particular sediment or rock type.

Moraine records glacial deposition; its presence does not establish the material beneath the former glacier or lagoon floor. The overlay therefore demonstrates differences in mapped foreland materials and coverage, while **buried substrate, sediment strength and relative erodibility remain unknown**. Further category and preparation details are in the [geology appendix](appendix.md#appendix-geology).

Longitudinal comparisons for Brown (left) and Stephenson (right), with distance measured inland from each route's seaward origin. **Row 1:** 2002 surface and modelled bed, smoothed with a three-station median; vertical markers locate dated fronts. ### A narrower surviving tongue and uneven flow support

Stephenson's former lower tongue was much broader than its surviving tongue. Its increasing recent front-displacement rate therefore occurs within a substantially changed footprint. The width curves measure mapped ice width; they do not establish the width of the underlying bedrock valley.

Filtered speeds are higher at Stephenson, but retained coverage is also much greater:

| 2016–2022 velocity summary within the 2014 footprint | Brown | Stephenson |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Whole-footprint median speed | 207.68 m/year | 281.75 m/year |
| Whole-footprint retained coverage | 64.71% | 91.51% |
| Lower-third median speed | 39.57 m/year | 266.52 m/year |
| Lower-third retained coverage | 38.46% | 85.41% |

The lower third means the **lowest third of each glacier's DEM-derived elevation range**, rather than one third of its area or length. Brown's lower-glacier median represents fewer than half of the eligible cells. Missing speeds do not indicate stationary ice, and the filter removes speeds at or below 10 m/year by design. These are medians of retained observations, not complete measurements of either glacier's flow.

Both longitudinal profiles include faster sections inland of the sampled lower reaches, followed by lower speeds near the inland endpoints. The 120 m velocity grid and its 2016–2022 period limit comparisons with earlier retreat; sampling every 50 m adds no independent spatial resolution. Filtering and sensitivity details are in the [velocity appendix](appendix.md#appendix-velocity).

### Candidate bed highs remain uncertain

The modelled bed contains local rises and dips beneath valid ice within the 2019 footprint. Brown's screened highs lie near **2.5 and 4.1 km** along its route; Stephenson's lie near **6.6 and 9.1 km**. None persists through every supported route-offset and smoothing test.

Cross-sections identify Brown's inland candidate, B2, as a local rise with lower modelled terrain on either side. Lateral support around Stephenson's S1 is insufficient to establish its continuity. **No candidate is demonstrated to be a valley-wide barrier or a pinning point.** Detailed screening and cross-sections are in the [terrain appendix](appendix.md#appendix-bed-highs).

The model does not cover the entire formerly occupied coastal tongue. It combines older surface elevations with modelled thickness, and the thickness estimation itself uses ice motion (Millan et al., 2022). Agreement between modelled terrain and flow is therefore not wholly independent evidence of a bed control.

## Summer orientation offers a smaller geometric contrast

:::{figure} Figures/09_Synthesis/04e_surface_exposure.png
:label: fig-results-exposure
:alt: Shared-colour-scale maps of geometric summer exposure across the 2014 Brown and Stephenson glacier footprints, with the 600-metre contour.
:width: 100%
:align: center

Summer incidence index derived from surface slope, aspect and hourly solar geometry on four representative November–February dates at 53.1°S. The 2014 footprints are sampled on a 120 m grid. **1.00** is the corresponding horizontal-surface value; purple indicates lower geometric exposure and gold higher exposure. White lines mark glacier outlines; pink contours mark 600 m, with labels identifying the lower side. Dark gaps have no valid index. Maps share a colour scale but use different zoom levels; end colours group values outside 0.90–1.10. This is geometric exposure, not measured radiation or melt. Terrain source: Brolsma and Smith (2008).
:::

Brown's whole-footprint median index is **1.028**, compared with **0.983 at Stephenson**: approximately 2.8% above and 1.7% below the horizontal-surface value. Below 600 m, the medians are **1.024** and **0.968**, respectively. Valid coverage exceeds **96%** in all four comparisons.

Brown thus has the slightly higher orientation index, while Stephenson has the greater cumulative area loss. The index does not reproduce the retreat ranking. It also omits cloud, atmospheric attenuation, diffuse radiation, albedo and shadows cast by surrounding terrain; such shadows can materially affect glacier radiation (Olson and Rupper, 2019). These values cannot establish which glacier received more energy or experienced more melt. Further calculation details are in the [exposure appendix](appendix.md#appendix-exposure).

## Brown Lagoon: the basin supported by the soundings

:::{figure} Figures/09_Synthesis/05a_Brown_basin_geometry.png
:label: fig-results-lagoon
:alt: Brown Lagoon supported bathymetry, three cross-basin depth profiles and an along-route depth profile, with portions of historical glacier outlines.
:width: 100%
:align: center

Brown Lagoon geometry reconstructed from the 2004 soundings. **A:** retained interpolated depths, the main analysis route, cross-sections L1–L3 and portions of the 1947 and 1988 outlines; blank areas lack supported depth estimates. **B:** cross-basin profiles, with zero offset at the main route. **C:** depth along that route, measured inland from its seaward origin. Depth increases downward in both profile panels and is relative to the 2004 survey water surface. Profile endpoints and raster edges do not establish shorelines. Field archive: Allison and Thost (2010); historical outlines: Donoghue and Harris (2021).
:::

The supported reconstruction covers **0.3301 km²**, with a deep central area and shallower flanks. Approximately **0.1519 km² (46%) is at least 30 m deep**, and **0.0474 km² (14%) is at least 50 m deep**. The interpolated maximum is **55.6 m**, close to the deepest observed sounding of **56 m**. The longitudinal profile reaches approximately 50 m, while the cross-sections reach roughly 49–54 m.

The entire supported footprint lies within the **1947 glacier outline**, with no overlap with the **1988, 2014, 2019 or 2026 outlines**. Mapped ice therefore withdrew from this surveyed area between 1947 and 1988. This agrees with the early lake-associated retreat described by Tielidze et al. (2025), but does not independently date formation of the whole lagoon or the end of every glacier–water contact.

These are measurements of the supported survey footprint, rather than the entire lagoon. Brown has **211 usable soundings**, compared with only **16 at Stephenson**, preventing an equivalent basin comparison. Spatial block validation yielded supported predictions for **119 of 211 withheld observations**, with **4.47 m RMSE** and **−1.95 m bias**: predictions were shallower on average. Those errors apply to the supported validation subset, not to unsurveyed margins. Coverage rules and sensitivity checks are in the [field reconstruction appendix](appendix.md#appendix-field).

## Lagoon depths and modelled glacier terrain: what can be connected?

:::{figure} Figures/09_Synthesis/05b_Brown_basin_to_glacier.png
:label: fig-results-lagoon-bed
:alt: Brown Lagoon bed elevations under three assumed water levels, compared with inland surface and modelled bed profiles, nearby interpreted radar sections and historical front markers.
:width: 100%
:align: center

Brown Lagoon and inland glacier terrain along the analysis route. **A:** 2002 surface elevation, smoothed modelled bed within the 2019 outline, and lagoon-bed elevations under assumed water levels of **0, 2 and 5 m** above approximate sea-level zero. Vertical lines mark dated fronts. Crosses are samples from interpreted 2000 radar-bed sections within 100 m of the route, projected onto it; they are not independent point soundings or exact on-route observations. **B:** enlarged lagoon profiles showing the scenario offsets. The zero line is an approximate elevation reference. Gaps remain between datasets, so the figure does not reconstruct a continuous connecting bed. Sources: Allison and Thost (2010), Brolsma and Smith (2008), Millan et al. (2022), and the glacier outlines described above.
:::

Changing the assumed water level shifts the lagoon-bed profile vertically by up to **5 m**, while preserving its shape. Its deep central part remains below approximate sea-level zero in all three scenarios; the modelled inland bed lies much higher. These chosen offsets demonstrate sensitivity to water level, without measuring its true value or bounding vertical-reference uncertainty.

The archived 2003 shoreline track provides **581 GPS positions**. Subtracting the project's approximate 40 m ellipsoidal-height correction gives a median near **0.64 m**, but this is a shoreline-track elevation summary, not an independently measured lagoon water level. It does not resolve the vertical alignment of all datasets.

The gap between bathymetry and the modelled inland bed remains unobserved. Nearby interpreted radar sections provide local context, with uneven model agreement: bed-elevation RMSE is approximately **62.1 m at BG30**, **25.5 m at BG45** and **10.5 m at BG50**, each spanning only two or three distinct model cells. These comparisons do not validate the full glacier bed.

The evidence establishes a deep surveyed depression formerly occupied by mapped ice, but leaves the **connecting bed, outlet sill and hydraulic connectivity unresolved**. It cannot identify seawater intrusion, quantify calving or water-driven melt, or demonstrate a continuous overdeepened basin extending beneath the remaining glacier.

(results-summary)=
## Evidence to carry into the discussion

1. **The retreat contrast depends on the measure and interval.** Stephenson lost more area overall, yet its recent front acceleration accompanied slower proportional area loss. Brown shows the opposite recent combination.
2. **The glaciers now occupy different geometries.** Stephenson lost most of its extensive low-elevation tongue and retains a narrower mapped tongue. Filtered flow is faster at Stephenson, while candidate bed controls remain model-dependent and incompletely supported.
3. **The strongest field evidence is local.** Brown's soundings establish a deep basin within its former ice footprint. Geological mapping and the missing basin-to-glacier connection leave the buried substrate and proposed mechanisms unresolved; the orientation index alone does not explain the retreat ranking.

The [evidence appendix](appendix.md#appendix-evidence) records supporting comparisons and limitations. Continue to [Discussion](discussion.md), where these observations are combined with the literature to assess possible explanations.
