Brown Glacier field report

Our goal for the 2000 field season was to characterize one of the glaciers on Heard Island, preferably a land terminating one, as they are more sensitive indicators of climate change. Brown Glacier near Spit camp was chosen, because it seemed like a simple enough glacier to work on.
	We set out to measure some basic glacier parameters such as ice velocity, ice thickness, some meteorological parameters, and ice accumulation. We also surveyed the glacier elevation, the terminus position, old moraines, and the shoreline and depth of the proglacial lagoon. In addition we were to put up a semi-permanent automatic weather station (AWS) at a suitable spot close to the glacier.
	To accomplish these goals we set up a separate field camp with one of the small field huts at the base of the glacier and a pyramid tent at about 600 m a.s.l., where the AWS was to be erected.
	Field operations went extremely well, and we are very pleased with the results of the field season. We acknowledge the full support from the field leader who granted us helicopter time whenever possible. The FTOs who worked with us in the field (Don Hudspeth and Paul Scott) were extremely helpful. We also appreciate the effort of Paul Scott and Andrew Lock to go back and remeasure some of our canes after we had left the island. The field hut worked very well, in fact much better than we had anticipated. 
	In the following I list some points, where we feel things could be improved. They shouldn’t deter from the fact that we are in general very pleased with the way operations went.

·	4 people are too many for a small hut for anything longer than a few nights. Condensation becomes a real problem. Some modifications in the huts might help, such as having ventilation at the apex, and lowering the upper bunks by 5-10 cm.
·	We were living almost exclusively on freeze dried food. At a camp like ours it would be easy to have a bit more variety. This is something we could have avoided by becoming involved in the food planning. We did buy some food ourselves, which made things a little bit better.
·	I have the impression that the Antarctic Division regards skis as a fun accessory rather than an important tool. I know I’m a bit biased, but for glacier work skis are much safer and far more efficient than walking on snow shoes. We were issued skis, but they were old, heavy, and the bindings on two of the three pairs broke.
·	It was very unfortunate that the aerial photography (a mapping program task) could not have been undertaken during the January - February period when weather conditions are know to be more suitable than October and ice extent is not obscured by seasonal snow cover. This photography is necessary for an overall assessment of current glacier extent. From the photography (aerial and on foot) that was taken, I think we have only about half of the island covered, and less than this with vertical aerial photography.
·	It was unfortunate that neither Spit Base and Brown Glacier Base did not have good radio communications with Atlas Cove. Several hours after the departure of V4 from Atlas Cove on the evening of 10 Nov., the Aurora was able to pick up and transmit to Brown Glacier and Spit during and after the evening radio sched quite loud and clear on both HF , whereas Atlas was barely audible despite it being virtually within line of sight. This would suggest that perhaps there were some problems with the Atlas Cove radio/antenna setup.
